## ⚠️ Three popular stories that are WRONG (and are the heart of chapters 08 and 10)

### 1. "Radioactivity is what proved Kelvin wrong about the age of the Earth"
**This is itself a myth.** The real flaw was that Kelvin modelled the Earth cooling by
**conduction alone**. His former student and assistant **John Perry** showed in
**1894–95** — *before radioactivity was discovered* — that if the deep interior can
**convect**, heat keeps arriving at the surface and the Earth can be vastly older.
Perry got **2–3 billion years**, close to the modern 4.54 Gy.

Radioactive heating is real but secondary: it accounts for roughly half of Earth's
present surface heat loss and does not by itself rescue the calculation. Perry's
critique was largely ignored — he revered Kelvin, wasn't a self-promoter, and
geologists read the exchange as an incomprehensible quarrel between physicists.

**Why this is great for the project:** the chapter becomes "Kelvin was wrong about the
age of the Earth — and almost everything you've been told about *why* is also wrong."
The interactive must therefore make **convection** the primary correction and
radioactivity the secondary one, not the reverse.

### 2. The Rutherford anecdote is real but is usually misapplied
Rutherford, Royal Institution, **20 May 1904**, in his own words:
> "I came into the room which was half-dark and presently spotted Lord Kelvin in the
> audience, and realised that I was in for trouble… To my relief, Kelvin fell fast
> asleep, but as I came to the important point, I saw the old bird sit up, open an eye
> and cock a baleful glance at me."

He then said Kelvin had limited the age of the Earth "provided no new source [of heat]
was discovered" — "that prophetic utterance refers to what we are now considering
tonight, radium!" **But that caveat was about Kelvin's calculation of the age of the
SUN, not the Earth's cooling.** Tell the anecdote — it's genuine and it's a great
story — but tell it accurately.

### 3. "There is nothing new to be discovered in physics now…"
**No primary source exists. Kelvin almost certainly never said it.** The nearest real
statement is **Albert A. Michelson, 1894**; Robert Millikan later guessed the
"eminent physicist" Michelson had in mind was Kelvin, and the misattribution stuck.
It is doubly wrong because Kelvin's actual 1900 position was the **opposite** — see
the two clouds below.

## Biographical (rubric §1)

- Born **26 June 1824, Belfast**, Ireland. Died **17 December 1907** at Netherhall,
  Largs, Scotland. Buried **Westminster Abbey, beside Newton**.
- Father **James Thomson** — a Ballynahinch farmer's son who became professor of
  mathematics, first in Belfast then at **Glasgow from 1832**. He taught his own
  children at home; that home schooling is the direct cause of the early start.
- Mother **Margaret Gardner** died when William was **six**.
- **Matriculated at the University of Glasgow at age 10** (the Scottish system then
  admitted very young students; he was not taking a degree at 10, he was attending).
- Read **Fourier's *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* at 16** and published his first
  paper that year defending Fourier against a critic, under the pseudonym **"P.Q.R."**
  Fourier's heat mathematics runs through his entire career — the Earth's cooling, the
  telegraph cable, and the tide machine are all Fourier.
- **Peterhouse, Cambridge, 1841–45**: **Second Wrangler**, but won the **Smith's
  Prize** (the examiner's line: the first wrangler would not be the better man). Also
  a serious athlete — won the **Colquhoun Sculls in 1843**.
- 1845: a year in **Henri Victor Regnault's laboratory in Paris**, learning
  experimental precision — the thing his Cambridge training lacked.
- **1846: elected Professor of Natural Philosophy at Glasgow at age 22.** Held the
  chair **53 years**, to 1899. Founded **Britain's first physics teaching laboratory**.

## World context (rubric §2)

- Victorian Britain at peak industrial and imperial power: steam, iron, railways, and
  a global telegraph network being built for commerce and empire.
- The word **"scientist" was only coined in 1833** (by William Whewell) — Kelvin's own
  title was *natural philosopher*. Science professionalised during his lifetime.
- **Great Exhibition 1851**; the **Irish famine 1845–52** in his birthplace; the
  **American Civil War** (interrupted cable work); **Darwin's *Origin* 1859**.
- **Necessity vs happenstance** — Kelvin is an unusually clean case study:
  - **Commercial necessity**: the transatlantic cable work, the mariner's compass, the
    sounding machine, the tide predictor. Investors needed them; he got rich (~70
    patents).
  - **Pure theory**: the absolute temperature scale, the Second Law statement — driven
    by Carnot and Fourier, not by anyone's money.
  - **Collaboration/happenstance**: the Joule–Thomson effect came out of a friendship
    with a Manchester brewer's son, **James Prescott Joule**, whom the establishment
    was ignoring when Kelvin met him at the 1847 BAAS meeting.

## Science (rubric §3) — and what was believed BEFORE

| Contribution | What people believed before |
|---|---|
| **Absolute temperature scale (1848)**, from Carnot's theory, independent of any working substance. Absolute zero = **−273.15 °C**. | Temperature scales were arbitrary and tied to a substance (mercury, alcohol). No agreed *lower bound* to temperature. |
| **Second Law of Thermodynamics — Kelvin statement (1851)**; "On a Universal Tendency in Nature to the Dissipation of Mechanical Energy" (1852) → the "heat death" idea. | Heat was **caloric**, a weightless fluid that flowed and was conserved. Energy was not yet a unifying concept. |
| **Joule–Thomson effect (1852)** with Joule: gas throttled through a porous plug cools below its inversion temperature. | Gases were assumed to behave ideally; no practical route to liquefying air. This became **refrigeration, liquid nitrogen, MRI magnets, and the whole cryogenics industry**. |
| **Transatlantic telegraph**: the theory of signal retardation in a long submerged cable (retardation ∝ **L²**, the "law of squares"), the **mirror galvanometer**, the **siphon recorder**. Failed cable 1858; success **1866**; **knighted 1866**. | Signals were assumed effectively instantaneous; Wildman Whitehouse insisted on brute high voltage, which destroyed the 1858 cable. Kelvin's answer was tiny voltages plus an absurdly sensitive detector. |
| **~70 patents**: Kelvin bridge (low resistances), mariner's compass (adopted by the Royal Navy), depth sounding machine, **tide-predicting machine** (a mechanical Fourier synthesiser — an analogue computer). | — |
| **Two clouds (1900)** | — see below |

**Awards/honours**: FRS **1851**; **Royal Medal 1856**; **Copley Medal 1883**;
**knighted 1866** (for the cable); **Baron Kelvin of Largs 1892** — the **first British
scientist raised to the House of Lords**; **President of the Royal Society 1890–95**;
**Order of Merit 1902**; Privy Counsellor 1902; 21 honorary doctorates. (No Nobel — the
prizes began in 1901 and he never received one.)

**Legacy today**: the **kelvin** is an SI base unit, and since the **2019
redefinition** it is fixed by the Boltzmann constant **k = 1.380649×10⁻²³ J/K exactly**
rather than by the triple point of water.

### The two clouds — 27 April 1900, Royal Institution Friday Evening Lecture
Title: *"Nineteenth Century Clouds over the Dynamical Theory of Heat and Light"*
(expanded version published in *Philosophical Magazine*, July 1901).

> "The beauty and clearness of the dynamical theory, which asserts heat and light to be
> modes of motion, is at present obscured by two clouds."

- **Cloud I** — how can the Earth move through the luminiferous ether? (the
  Michelson–Morley null result) → became **special relativity, 1905**.
- **Cloud II** — the Maxwell–Boltzmann equipartition of energy failing (specific heats,
  blackbody radiation) → became **quantum mechanics** (Planck 1900, Einstein 1905).

Both clouds produced a revolution within five years. This is the single best rebuttal
to the fake "nothing left to discover" quote.

## Personal (rubric §4)

- **Restless, digressive lecturer** who regularly abandoned the syllabus for whatever
  he was researching that week; filled **100+ working notebooks** over his life.
- **Sailor**: bought the 126-ton schooner yacht ***Lalla Rookh*** (from 1870) and spent
  summers aboard; many of his later patents are maritime because of it.
- **Musician** — played the French horn. **Athlete** — champion sculler at Cambridge.
- **Married twice, no children.** First **Margaret Crum** (1852), his childhood
  sweetheart, in poor health for most of their marriage; she died 1870. Second
  **Frances ("Fanny") Blandy** of Madeira, whose family he met because the cable ships
  put in at Funchal — they married in 1874 **on his 50th birthday**.
- ⚠️ **Correction to a common claim**: it was his **Glasgow house** (Professors' Square,
  University of Glasgow) that was **the first house in Britain lit by electric light,
  in 1881** — not Netherhall, the Largs mansion he built in 1874 and died in.
- **Philosophy of science** — two pillars:
  1. **Measurement is knowledge.** "When you can measure what you are speaking about,
     and express it in numbers, you know something about it; but when you cannot
     measure it… your knowledge is of a meagre and unsatisfactory kind." (*Electrical
     Units of Measurement*, lecture, 3 May 1883.)
  2. **Mechanical models or it isn't understanding.** He famously could not accept a
     theory until he could build a mechanical model of it — which is also why he
     resisted Maxwell's abstract field theory, and why he spent years on the
     (wrong, but productive) **vortex atom** idea, which seeded modern **knot theory**.
- **What was unusual**: a theoretician who got rich from patents; a Lord who kept
  lecturing; wrong in public for forty years about the Earth's age and gracious about
  it at the end; devout, and explicitly used his physics against Darwinian timescales.
- **Contemporary peers**: **James Prescott Joule** (collaborator), **James Clerk
  Maxwell** (fellow Scot, correspondent), **Hermann von Helmholtz** (close friend),
  **George Gabriel Stokes** (lifelong correspondent, ~650 letters), **Peter Guthrie
  Tait** (co-author of *Treatise on Natural Philosophy*, "T&T′"), **Michael Faraday**,
  **Rudolf Clausius** (rival on thermodynamics), **Charles Darwin** (antagonist — Darwin
  called Kelvin "an odious spectre"), **Cyrus Field** (the cable's financier),
  **Wildman Whitehouse** (the cable's antagonist), **John Perry** (the student who was
  right), **Ernest Rutherford** (the young man at the 1904 lecture).

## Other wrong predictions (chapter 10, with verdicts)
- **"Heavier-than-air flying machines are impossible"** — **substantially real.** In an
  1896 letter declining to join the Aeronautical Society he wrote he had "not the
  smallest molecule of faith in aerial navigation other than ballooning."
- **"X-rays will prove to be a hoax"** — **unsupported.** Kelvin had his own hand
  X-rayed in 1896 and wrote to congratulate Röntgen.
